Basically, there are a few main rules and situations in which a comma is needed. And also feel free to put a comma anywhere to prevent misreading)

# Separating Independent Clauses

An independent clause contains a subject and a verb and can also exist alone as a complete sentence. A comma is used before a coordinating conjunction, i.e., FANBOYS[^1].

*We absolutely have to banish factory farming, so we will do our best to make that moment closer.*

But if there are 2 relatively short clauses (<4-5 words each), the comma can be omitted: *I hate you and you somehow still like me.*

# Dependent First, Independent Second

When the indepedent clause comes first, you generally don't need a comma. Otherwise, you do need. 

*We must buy coffee because it's running out.*
*Because coffee is running out, we must buy it.*

But if the dependent clause starts with a word showing contrast (although, even though, whereas), use a comma.

*We should invest in radical life extention, although it will require significant resources.*

Also, when the if-clause (dependent) comes before independent one, you need a comma.

*If you reverse the sentence and put the "if" clasue in the beginning, you must use a comma.*

# Following Introductory Elements

It can be either:
1. Introductory word:
	- *Furthermore, we also need to stop global warming.*  
2. Introductory phrase:
	- *As you can see, we are doing not so well.* 
3. Introductory dependent clause:
	- *If I want you to understand me, I must consider being less weird.*

# Separating Items In a Series

Used when there are 3 or more words. A comma is also used before the last "and" (Oxford comma); it is preferred but can be omitted.

*I like soft pink, beige, white, and moss green.*

# Enclosing Non-Essential Elements

A comma is used to enclose words, phrases, and clauses that are not essential in the sentence and can be omitted without losing the main meaning.

*Kate Tolo, a famous Blueprint co-founder, is going to become the most measured female in the world.*

Example with no comma (where all parts are essential): *A famous Blueprint co-founder Kate Tolo is going to become the most measured female in the world.*

# Separating Coordinate Adjectives

If 2 or more adjectives modify the same noun equally, we should use a comma. To understand if they really do it equally, just reverse their order or place "and" between them. If it sounds natural, you need a comma. If it sounds weird, you do not need a comma. 

*You are a beautiful, stunning, hot, and gorgeous princess.*

Example with no comma: *I just got those new black jeans.*

# Direct Address And Quotations

*Lola, how are you today?*
*I said, "Who the f are you? I'm alone in this room".*
*They replied, "Guess who forgot to take their pills again", and laughed.*

# Separating Contrasting Parts

A comma is used before "not".

*I want you to become an engineer, not just to finish high school.*

# Tag Questions And Mild Interjections

*It was such an amazing day, wasn't it?*
*Yes, it absolutely was!*

# Absolute Phrases

An absolute phrase contains a subject and a participle (verb + ing/ed). Basically, it is a regular sentence from which auxiliary verbs (was/were/is/are/am) were excluded. It is not grammatically attached to the main sentence with a conjunction but adds additional information to it.

*The activists stood on the main street, their signs demanding peace.* 
*Their sleep schedule optimized to 8 hours per night, the participants showed a 22% improvement in daily cognitive function.*

[^1]: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so
